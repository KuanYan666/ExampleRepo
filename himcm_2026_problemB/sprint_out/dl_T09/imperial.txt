The boundary between random and non-random passenger arrivals:
      Robust empirical evidence and economic implications
         Ramandeep Singh∗, Daniel J. Graham, Daniel Hörcher, Richard J. Anderson
              Transport Strategy Centre, Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering
                                Imperial College London, United Kingdom




Abstract
    In this paper, we investigate the influence of train headways on passenger platform wait
times using automated data from the London Underground metro system. For high fre-
quency services, the literature suggests that passenger arrivals are random and that under
perfectly random conditions with all other factors held constant, wait times are equivalent
to half of the headway between trains. We test this hypothesis using large-scale smart card
and vehicle location data, which enables the extraction of access times from total passenger
journey times as well as the precise measurement of train headways. Using a semipara-
metric regression modelling framework, we generate non-linear estimates of the relationship
between access times and headway while conditioning for other service supply and demand
factors. Marginal platform wait times are then derived numerically via an exposure-response
model framework which accounts for potential confounding between the walking and waiting
components of access times, thus enabling quantification of the unbiased impact of headways
on wait times. For three lines in central London, we observe that marginal wait times tran-
sition from greater than half of the headway to approximately one third of the headway as
train frequencies decrease. The transition occurs in the range between 2-3 minute headways,
lower than earlier estimates in the literature. A series of numerical simulations illustrate the
importance of waiting time sensitivity in the optimisation of public transport services. In
comparison with the standard wait time assumption, our exercise reveals that the degree of
density economies is milder than what the literature suggests, and this may neutralise some
of the economic justifications of high public transport subsidies.
Keywords: Platform wait times, AFC data, AVL data, Revealed preference,
Semiparametric regression


1. Introduction
   The time spent waiting at a station platform prior to boarding is considered by passengers
to be one of the most inconvenient phases of a transit journey. In terms of the generalised

cost of travel, the value of platform wait time is typically weighted at least twice as much
as the value of uncrowded in-vehicle time (Wardman et al., 2016). Transport for London
applies a higher weighting of 2.5 for wait time, compared to a base weighting of 1 allocated
for in-vehicle time in uncrowded conditions (TfL, 2013). Wait time therefore incurs at least
double the amount of disutility to passengers as when they are travelling on empty trains.
Since wait times are such an influential component of passenger perceptions of transit travel,
understanding and quantifying passenger wait time patterns is essential.
    Train frequency is a primary determinant of passenger wait time (Newell, 1982; Osana
and Newell, 1972). In the literature, the consensus is that passenger arrival patterns are
influenced by train frequency levels as follows: (i) when train frequencies are high, passengers
arrive in a random manner, resulting in an average wait time equivalent to half of the
headway between trains, and (ii) at lower service frequencies, passengers tend to arrive in
a non-random manner in order to minimise their wait times. In the most recent empirical
work on rail transit covering both high frequency metro and low-frequency suburban rail
modes, the transition from random to non-random passenger arrivals is reported to occur
in the range between 5 and 11 minute headways (Berggren et al., 2019; Ingvardson et al.,
2018; Fan and Machemehl, 2009; Luethi et al., 2007). With increasing access to advanced
train arrival information available through open source data online and via mobile phone
applications, the transition to timed passenger arrivals may occur at shorter headways.
    In this paper, the prevailing hypothesis that passenger wait times transition from ran-
dom to non-random as headways increase is tested, using the London Underground metro
system as a case study. Automated fare collection (AFC) data from the Oyster card system
and automated vehicle location (AVL) data on train movements are used, and these data
combined provide a revealed preference framework with a very large sample size. Three
parts of analysis are undertaken to establish the effect of headways on passenger wait times
at the origin station as follows:

  1. Total passenger journey times are decomposed via a probabilistic passenger to train
     assignment algorithm to calculate passenger access time, i.e, the time taken from
     passenger tap-in to train boarding at the origin station.
  2. Semiparametric regression modelling is undertaken to quantify the relationship be-
     tween headways and access times while conditioning on other service supply and pas-
     senger demand characteristics.
  3. The effect of headways on marginal passenger wait times is then isolated and quantified
     via an exposure-response model framework.

   Marginal passenger wait times with respect to train headways are calculated in a two-
step process. First, expected values of access time at different values of train headway
are calculated from the outputs of the semiparametric regression model of access times.
This generates a relationship which can be considered analogous to a exposure-response
function, a model framework commonly applied in the medical toxicology literature (Haschek
et al., 2013; Wang, 2015). In this case, access time is the response and headway is the
exposure. Second, the derivative of access time with respect to headway is calculated via

                                               2
finite differencing. This quantity is used to infer marginal wait time with respect to headway.
Through this process, passenger arrival patterns are able to be characterised and the point
at which arrivals transition from random to non-random across three high frequency lines
in central London is established.
    In the literature, the majority of models exploring the relationship between wait times
and headway rely on direct measurement of wait times through the observation of samples
of boarding passengers at station platforms or from passenger stated preference survey data.
Parametric functional forms are assigned to the wait time data, and inferences are then
made regarding passenger arrival and wait time patterns (Luethi et al., 2007; Nygaard and
Torset, 2016). Separate analyses may then be undertaken to quantify the effect of different
supply and demand factors; linear regression modelling and analysis of variance (ANOVA)
statistical testing methods are the most prevalent analysis techniques (Fan and Machemehl,
2009; Tavassoli et al., 2018; Berggren et al., 2019).
    In comparison with the existing empirical literature on wait times, the methodological
contribution of the present paper is threefold. First, we use large-scale automated data sets
from which access times are derived in a passenger-to-train assignment framework. This
makes data collection more feasible from a time and cost perspective, while the potential
bias of small-scale manual sampling is eliminated. Second, the application of semiparametric
regression rather than the conventional linear regression methods enables more robust esti-
mation of parameter coefficients which have a greater degree of fidelity to the data. Third,
the paper contributes with the derivation of a novel exposure-response functional framework
to infer marginal wait times with respect to headway. Within this framework, we quantify
the unbiased impact of headways on wait times via the derivation of marginal wait times to
account for potential confounding between the walking and waiting components of access
times. Our study demonstrates that the numerical approximation of marginal wait times
generates valid results for the London Underground metro system, and so the method can
be readily applied to other systems to analyse wait time patterns from routinely collected
automated data.
    Our study also investigates the application of wait time modelling at the supply side
of public transport provision. Far-reaching conclusions are derived regarding the economic
implications of replacing the standard assumption of random passenger arrivals with ro-
bust econometric estimates of the marginal wait time–headway relationship. We show that
the simplifying assumption of random arrivals leads to the underestimation of the socially
optimal frequency in high-demand periods and overestimation in the off-peak. Moreover,
with our new estimates, density economies in the operator costs of service provision tend
to disappear, thus neutralising some of the economic justifications for high public transport
subsidies.
    In terms of the applicability of this study on the demand side of public transport, a
number of studies suggest that the provision of real-time information improves passenger
perceptions of wait times at transit stops (Dziekan and Kottenhoff, 2007; Watkins et al.,
2011; Brakewood et al., 2014a,b). The results of this study reveal the instances where real-
time information on train arrivals might be the most useful for passengers. Around the
critical headway (approx 2-3 mins), such information is decisive in whether passengers are
                                              3
willing to risk a non-random arrival. This information could be communicated to passengers,
and could ultimately improve passenger perceptions of service quality.
    The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: a literature review of work on the
estimation of transit platform wait times is presented in Section 2. The study area and
data are briefly summarised in Section 3. The methods for the passenger to train assign-
ment algorithm, the semiparametric regression modelling framework, and the estimation
of marginal wait times are detailed in Section 4, followed by the results and discussion in
Section 5. Conclusions and suggestions for future work are given in Section 6.

2. Literature review
2.1. Fundamental properties of transit wait times
    The earliest work on estimating wait times for rail transit was based on work undertaken
for bus transit, where researchers made the assumption that passengers arrive randomly, and
the resulting wait times follow a uniform distribution (Holroyd and Scraggs, 1966; Osana
and Newell, 1972; Abkowitz and Tozzi, 1987).
    The seminal formula for platform wait time is:

     E[w] = 0.5E[h](1 + cv 2h ),                                                         (1)

where w denotes wait time, h is train headway between consecutive services, and cvh is the
coefficient of variation of headway (Newell, 1982; Osana and Newell, 1972). The formula
expects that passengers tend to arrive randomly, and so wait times are a function of half of
the headway between trains including an adjustment for the regularity of headways.
   O’Flaherty and Mangan (1970), Seddon and Day (1974), Jolliffe and Hutchinson (1975),
Turnquist (1978), and Bowman and Turnquist (1981) were among the first researchers to
observe that although some passengers do indeed arrive randomly, others use knowledge of
schedules via published timetables or experience to adjust their arrival to minimise wait
times. In subsequent work on wait times, researchers generally adopt the two-category
typology of passenger wait times proposed by Turnquist (1978):

  1. Passengers arrive randomly when service frequencies are high and resulting expected
     wait times are low.
  2. Passengers arrive non-randomly and time their arrival to minimise wait time when
     service frequencies are low and train schedule information is known.

   When passenger arrivals are perfectly random with a uniform distribution, the expected
wait time E[w] is half of the headway, i.e. E[w] = 0.5h. When passenger arrivals are a
mix of random and non-random, it follows that the expected wait time can be assumed
to be less than half of the headway. Fosgerau (2009) and Fosgerau and Engelson (2011)
document the theoretical foundations of the boundary between random and non-random
passenger arrivals. In terms of empirical estimates of the transition point, the US Transit
Capacity and Service Quality Manual currently quotes the transition at 10 minute headways
(Kittelson & Associates Inc et al., 2013). Other empirical research on bus and rail modes
                                             4
quote values as low as 5 minutes; a brief review of relevant studies are presented in the
following sections.
    O’Flaherty and Mangan (1970) characterise bus wait times in Leeds, UK, as a linear
function of headway, where the expected wait time is defined as E[w] = 1.79 + 0.14h.
Substituting E[w] = 0.5, the distinction between random and non-random arrivals occurs
at 5 minute headways. Seddon and Day (1974) analyse bus wait times in Manchester,
UK, and also observe a linear function where E[w] = 2.34 + 0.26h and the transition from
random to non-random arrivals occurs at approximately 10 minute headways. In more recent
work, Nygaard and Torset (2016) study bus travel in Trondheim, Norway and observe non-
random arrivals behaviour for bus services with 10, 15, and 20 minute headways. For the
10 minute headway service, the authors specify a linear model for the expected wait time,
where E[w] = 0.32h. In their study of bus wait times in Austin, Texas, Fan and Machemehl
(2009) analyse services with headways ranging from 8 to 60 minutes and also specify a linear
model for wait times, where E[w] = 2 + 0.3h. The transition from random to non-random
arrivals behaviour is reported to occur at 11 minute headways, and it is reported that after
a headway of 38 minutes, all arrivals can be considered as non-random.
    Two of the most recent studies on rail travel by Luethi et al. (2007) and Ingvardson
et al. (2018) use two-part mixture distributions to represent the random and non-random
arrivals. Luethi et al. (2007) analyse platform wait times for bus, tram, and rail in Zurich
Switzerland, and propose a mixed uniform and Johnson SB distribution. The transition from
random to non-random arrivals is observed to occur at approximately 5 minute headways.
Ingvardson et al. (2018) study wait times for metro and regional rail lines in Copenhagen with
headways ranging from 2 to 60 minutes, and propose a mixed uniform and beta distribution
of passenger arrivals. This distribution is tested against the Luethi et al. (2007) mixed
uniform and Johnson SB function, and is found to provide a better fit to the wait time data.
In terms of the passenger arrivals process, the authors find that the proportion of passengers
that exhibit non-random arrival behaviour is estimated at 43% at 5 minute headways, and
52% at 10 minute headways, and so it can be inferred that the transition between random
and non-random arrivals occurs between 5 and 10 minute headways. Berggren et al. (2019)
observe passenger wait times through the use of a mobile phone application for rail and
bus trips in Lund and Malmö, Sweden. The study finds that passengers show evidence of
non-random arrival behaviour at 5 minute headways.

2.2. Factors that influence wait times
    In line with the seminal formula for wait times for high frequency services widely adopted
by operators (refer to equation 1), empirical research verifies that the degree of headway
regularity also impacts wait times. Most studies report that waiting times become longer
as services become more irregular. Furth and Muller (2006) evaluate waiting costs for short
and long headway scenarios while incorporating an allowance for headway reliability, and
establish the transition between random and non-random arrivals by equating short headway
waiting cost with long headway waiting cost. Using six different operational scenarios, it is
shown that waiting costs tend to increase as headways become more irregular. Bowman and
Turnquist (1981) further confirm that for observed bus wait times in Chicago, passengers
                                              5
are more sensitive to the regularity of headways rather than headway itself. Frumin and
Zhao (2012) assess wait times on the London Overground rail lines, and find that passengers
arrive more randomly when the schedule deviation of the service is high. Nygaard and Torset
(2016) assess bus wait times in Trondheim, Norway, and show that passengers experience
longer wait times as the deviation between actual and scheduled arrivals increases.
    In addition to the influence of headway and headway regularity, empirical work shows
that passenger wait times are also influenced by the availability of train timetable infor-
mation, and other passenger-specific behaviours. Here a brief summary of the some of the
recent work in this area is presented.
    Ingvardson et al. (2018), Luethi et al. (2007), Frumin and Zhao (2012), and Csikos and
Currie (2007) directly and indirectly investigate the impact of passenger information on
wait times. Ingvardson et al. (2018) assess rail travel in Copenhagen, Luethi et al. (2007)
study bus, tram, and rail modes in Zurich, Frumin and Zhao (2012) study the London
Overground rail lines, and Csikos and Currie (2007) investigate rail travel in Melbourne,
Australia. The four studies investigate the impact of the time of day and all four report that
a higher proportion of passengers arrive randomly in off-peak hours than during the peak.
It is argued that those who travel in the peak are more likely to be regular travellers who
have knowledge of the train departures, and are therefore more likely to time their arrivals.
Ingvardson et al. (2018) also find that a greater proportion of passengers arrive randomly
for the metro service, which operates to a set service frequency rather than to a scheduled
timetable. In comparison to the random metro arrivals, they observe that passengers time
their arrival to minimise wait times for the rail lines where published timetables are available.
This illustrates the direct impact of passenger information on wait times; where available,
passengers will consult information to time their arrivals in order to reduce wait times.
    Passenger-specific behaviours are found to influence wait times in a number of studies.
Fan and Machemehl (2009) analyse wait time for buses in Austin, Texas, and perform a
linear regression analysis using OLS fitting to investigate the influence of headway, headway
regularity, time of day, and individual-specific characteristics including gender, ethnicity, and
access mode. The variables found to be significant at 95% are headway, headway regularity,
the dummy variable for the off-peak period, and the dummy variable for ethnicity type. All
significant variables have positive coefficients and so increases in the value of all variables
lead to longer wait times. Berggren et al. (2019) perform linear regression and ANOVA
tests on wait times for bus and rail modes in Sweden and observe that individual-specific
traits including gender, access mode, trip purpose influence passenger arrival times. Another
passenger-specific arrival preference is explored by Leurent and Xie (2018). It is asserted
that passengers opt to have longer wait times at the origin station in order to optimally
position themselves on the train such that their walking distance at the destination platform
is minimised. The method is applied to the Paris metro system using a similar maximum
likelihood optimisation algorithm as presented in Xie and Leurent (2017).
    Csikos and Currie (2008) investigate passenger-specific behaviours in more depth by
observing and categorising passengers according to their arrival preferences. The authors
extend on their earlier work on rail wait times in Melbourne, Australia (Csikos and Currie,
2007) and conduct a longitudinal study to analyse the arrival behaviour of regular passengers
                                               6
who complete the same route for at least 10 weekdays. Four types of arrivals behaviour is
defined: 1) those who have the greatest consistency in arrival time and arrive within a
narrow window (1-3 minutes before scheduled service), 2) those who arrive randomly but
within a consistent wider window of arrival time, 3) those who arrive consistently within a
given window, but also display outlying arrival times, and 4) random arrivals. The authors
find that passengers with the most consistent and narrow arrival window also exhibit the
shortest offset to the train arrival time (i.e. passenger arrival coincides very closely to train
arrival), and these passengers also tend to travel earlier in the peak period of the day.
    Tavassoli et al. (2018) investigate the impact of a wide range of different factors on
passenger wait times for rail travel in Brisbane, Australia. Using a log-logistic accelerated
failure time model framework, wait times are assumed to follow a log-logistic distribution
and model parameter estimates are assigned a linear form. Model covariates found to be
significant include: the time of day (AM peak, PM peak, and other times), operational
factors such as headway, the volume of passenger boardings and alightings, the number
of platforms, the location of the station, demographic characteristics including population
counts and employment, and trip characteristics such as transfer details (if applicable), and
if an alternative route is available.
    With the exception of Ingvardson et al. (2018), Tavassoli et al. (2018) and Frumin and
Zhao (2012), the majority of work on the characterisation of wait time patterns involves col-
lecting wait time data through stated preference surveys or through observation of passengers
at stations. Distribution fitting or linear regression methods are then typically adopted to
investigate the relationship between wait times and train headways. Studies that assess the
impact of additional supply and demand factors aside from headway predominately involve
the application of linear regression analysis methods.
    In this paper, rather than measuring or obtaining wait time estimates from stated pref-
erence sources, the impact of headways on passenger wait times is numerically derived
using revealed preference data. A probabilistic passenger to train assignment algorithm is
applied to first calculate the access time component of passenger trips from the revealed
preference data on total passenger journey times and train movements on the London Un-
derground. The non-linear semiparametric regression method is then applied to estimate
access times conditional on headways and other supply and demand characteristics of the
system. Marginal wait times as a function of headway are then numerically derived, enabling
an analysis of passenger wait time patterns at different train service frequencies.

2.3. Applications of wait time modelling
    In various fields of transport science, the standard assumption that wait time is equivalent
to half of the headway is typically applied, and so replacing this standard assumption has
a number of implications. Demand prediction and policy appraisal is among the most
straightforward applications. Interventions that affect the frequency of service provision
have an impact on waiting times as well, and empirical evidence shows that public transport
demand is highly sensitive to this journey attribute (Paulley et al., 2006). The unit value
of waiting time is significantly higher than the value of standard in-vehicle travel time; the
corresponding multipliers reach 1.4–1.8 in the literature (Wardman, 2004). If the impact
                                               7
of increased frequency on demand is significantly higher (lower) than the standard wait
time assumption predicts, then the corresponding response on the demand side might be
substantially underestimated (overestimated) by demand models. This implies that the
benefit side of such interventions is also underestimated (overestimated) in policy appraisal,
thus leading to biased recommendations in project ranking.
    A similarly influential application of wait time models is in short-run supply optimisation.
Waiting time is one of the key cost components responding to the frequency of service. The
trade-off between the operator costs on the one hand and the waiting time savings on the
benefit side is in the heart of supply modelling since the seminal contribution of Mohring
(1972). The marginal waiting time saving has an especially relevant role here, as even the
most recent literature still relies on the assumption that it equals to one half (for example,
Jara-Dı́az and Gschwender (2003); Hörcher and Graham (2018)).

3. Study area and data
    Selected sections of the Central, Jubilee, and Victoria lines on the London Underground
are analysed as follows: the entire length of the Victoria line (16 stations); West Acton to
Oxford Circus on the Central line (12 stations); and Bond Street to North Greenwich on
the Jubilee line (10 stations). The experimental area is depicted in Figure 1. We select
origin-destination pairs on each line that represent single-line trips only, excluding transfers,
and the line sections are chosen such that route choice decisions do not need to be made
under regular operating conditions. Since the line sections cover some of the busiest routes
in central London, the passenger data capture a mix of regular work commuters, school
children, tourists, and others.
    Passenger trip data and train movement data over a period of 7 weeks from October to
December in 2013 are used, focusing on weekday travel (Monday to Friday) only. The pas-
senger trip data are recorded via the Oyster smart card system, and include the timestamps
and locations of each trip between the origin and destination stations. The train movement
data are recorded by the internal TfL NetMIS system and include departure timestamps and
locations of each train movement at each station platform. The train movement data are
used to calculate the headways associated with each train trip; headways are calculated as
the difference between departure timestamps of consecutive train services at a given station
platform.

4. Methods
   The analysis involves three main parts. First, journey times from the AFC data set
report the total journey times only, and so to enable the journey times to be split into parts,
passengers must be allocated to trains. This is achieved by merging the AFC trip data with
the AVL train movement data and applying a probabilistic train assignment algorithm,
as presented in section 4.1. Second, a semiparametric regression model is developed to
quantify the relationship between access times and headway while conditioning for other
potentially influential supply and demand factors, as detailed in section 4.2. Third, the
unbiased relationship between access times and headway as previously quantified is used
to generate an average exposure-response model of access times, with headway as the sole
exposure variable. Two exposure-response models are generated: (i) environmental which
represents the relationship between access times and headway including the effect of all
other supply/demand covariates, and (ii) base which represents the relationship between
access time and headway only. The exposure-response models are then used to compute the
derivative of access times with respect to headway, and this quantity represents marginal
platform wait times. Environmental and base models of marginal platform wait times with
respect to headway are derived, as presented in section 4.3. For clarity, a summary of the
modelling framework including references to relevant equations is illustrated in Figure 2

4.2. Access time regression model
    Semiparametric regression is undertaken to quantify the relationship between access
times and headway. To ensure that the headway covariate is not acting as a proxy for
other factors, we also condition for a number of potentially influential supply and demand
characteristics. This model then enables the isolation of the sole impact of headways on
access times, which is then subsequently used to quantify the impact of headways on arrival
patterns.
    The advantage of the semiparametric regression method is that the relationships between
the independent and dependent variables are defined via basis functions generated from the
data, therefore enabling non-linearities and potential discontinuities to be modelled (Wood,
2006). The basis functions are penalised thin plate regression splines, which are interpolation
functions that are assigned to match the observed data points with a penalty applied to
capture the trade-off between the degree to which the spline functions match the data and
the degree of smoothness. The modelling is undertaken using the R statistical software
package ‘mgcv’. The models are fitted using penalised iteratively reweighted least squares
                                                        12
(PIRLS) and the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) technique is used to estimate the
model parameters (Wood et al., 2015; Wood, 2006).
     For each passenger trip i assigned to train itinerary j, an access time regression model can

Vij , and estimated model parameters θ.  b The headway and other service supply and demand

                                                        13
4.2.1. Supply side covariates
    Headway - To accommodate differing demand levels, the headways between trains vary
across times of the day and across locations. As discussed in the literature review, headway
is the primary determinant of platform wait times, and in turn, passenger access times. In
the model, the headway for each trip corresponds to the train the passenger boarded as
assigned via the passenger to train assignment algorithm, and is measured in minutes.
    Coefficient of variation of headway - In line with the seminal formula for platform wait
times presented in the literature review, the COV of headway is included in the access time
model to capture headway regularity. The COV of headway is calculated over a 15 minute
period for each OD pair.
    Headway normalised by mean headway - This covariate is included to capture potential
effects of train bunching. Train bunching refers to the condition where headways become
shorter as a result of longer train dwell times at stations. Bunching is more likely to occur
under congested conditions, where longer dwell times are required for higher volumes of
passenger boardings and/or alightings. The value is calculated as the headway associated
with the given trip normalised by the mean value of headways over the corresponding 15
minute period for each OD pair.
    Time of day - This covariate captures any unobserved temporal variation in the model.
A total of 72 categories are modelled to represent four 15 minute intervals per hour over
the operating period from 6am to 12am. A fixed effects form is assigned to accommodate
potential correlation with the other dynamic covariates in the model.
    Station and line effects - A set of static covariates capture any unobserved group-specific
effects of lines and stations. There are 35 stations and 6 combinations of the three lines in
two directions of travel. These effects are modelled as fixed effects, thus accommodating
potential correlation with the other station and line-based covariates in the models.

5. Results
5.1. Access time regression model
    The following sections present the results of the access time regression model. First, the
data cleaning process is summarised, second, the modelling process to reach the final model
form is presented, and third, the results of the significant covariate elasticities are discussed.
                                                  17
5.1.1. Data
    The raw data set including all outliers was initially modelled, and for a number of
covariates, outlying values were observed at the extremities. The outliers were therefore
discarded, and the resulting ranges of the data set are summarised in Table A.7 in Appendix
A.
    Due to the numerous covariates in the model, the filtered data set is tested for the
presence of linear and monotonic correlations through the Pearson and Spearman correlation
tests, respectively. The maximum values of the correlation coefficients are observed between
headway and normalised headway. The Spearman correlation coefficient has a maximum
value of 0.76 and the Pearson correlation coefficient has a maximum value of 0.71. In
correlation testing, the interpretation of the degree of association between two variables is
context dependent; a correlation coefficient of 0.85-0.90 would indicate a strong correlation in
cases where there are many data points (Kutner et al., 2005; Aldrich, 1995). In this analysis,
there are approximately 5 million data points, and so we can infer that the correlations are
not strong. It is therefore appropriate to include all covariates in the model. Moreover, we
highlight that potential collinearity issues would be detected in the regression modelling,
but no such issues are observed in the final model output.

5.1.2. Model form
    As previously presented in the regression model framework, the continuous covariates
can be modelled as either non-parametric smooth terms or as parametric terms, and the
categorical factors are modelled with group-specific effects. All continuous variables are ini-
tially modelled with non-parametric smooths, and if the functions are found to approximate
known parametric forms, a parametric form is assigned for tractability and model parsimony.
For the categorical factors capturing group effects, either a fixed or random effects structure
can be assigned. The fixed effects structure allows for the group effects to be correlated with
the other covariates in the model, while the random effects do not allow for correlation with
other covariates (Searle et al., 1992). From a theoretical standpoint, a fixed effects structure
is most appropriate for the group effects as there are many covariates concurrently capturing
similar characteristics of the system, and this could result in a degree of correlation between
covariates. For verification purposes, however, a random effects structure is also trialled.
    The results of five model forms are presented in Table A.8 in Appendix A to demonstrate
the model selection process. Model 1 represents a basic reference model with access time
as the response and headway as the sole covariate, which is modelled with non-parametric
smooths. The remaining models trialled include all additional supply/demand covariates
as follows: Model 2 - All continuous covariates modelled with non-parametric smooths
and fixed group effects, Model 3 - All continuous covariates modelled with non-parametric
smooths and random group effects, Model 4 - All continuous covariates modelled with linear
relationships and fixed group effects, and Model 5 - Final model form with parametric
and smooth continuous covariates and fixed group effects. To evaluate the performance of
the models, four criteria are used (Wood, 2006): the adjusted coefficient of determination
   2
(Radj. ), the percentage of explained model deviation (Dexplained ), the Akaike Information
Criterion (AIC), and the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) score. It should be noted
                                              18
that the Hausman test is conventionally used to compare the fixed and random effects
structures for simple linear panel models, however, this test is not able to be applied to the
semiparametric models generated here. As such, the performance of the fixed and random
effects are evaluated through the goodness-of-fit indicators.
    According to the goodness-of-fit results, all models with additional supply/demand co-
variates outperform the reference model with headway only, as expected, thus indicating the
benefit of including additional supply/demand covariates alongside headway. The difference
between the fixed and random effects structures (models 2 and 3) is not perceptible, and so
in keeping with the theoretical justification, a fixed effects structure is used. In models 2 to
5, for the form of the continuous covariates, the goodness-of-fit of the fully non-parametric
model form 2 is best while the linear model form 4 performs worst. In developing the fi-
nal model form, a balance between maximising the goodness-of-fit while maintaining model
parsimony and tractability is desired. As such, the final model form 5 performs better than
the simple linear model form 4, and in line with the fully non-parametric model form 2.
    To verify that the relationship between access times and headway is robust to model form,
we illustrate the relationship for each trialled model form 1 to 5 in Figure A.10 in Appendix
A. In Table A.8, headway is significant at a level of α = 0.001 in all models. As shown
in Figure A.10, all models illustrate an approximately monotonically increasing relationship
between access time and headway. Models 2, 3, and 5 (final form), which include additional
supply/demand covariates, all generate equivalent forms, thus indicating the robustness of
the access time and headway relationship when other covariates are assigned different forms.
As discussed previously, the linear model form (model 4) oversimplifies the relationship;
Figure A.10 shows that it does not capture the slight flattening of the slope at higher values
of headway. In model 1 (headway as the sole covariate), there is a plateau at headway
values of 1.6-2.0 minutes, while models 2, 3, and 5 do not have the same form. The highest
scheduled operating frequency of trains within the study area is 35 trains per hour on the
Central line, and this corresponds to a minimum headway of approximately 1.67 minutes
between trains. Approximately 15% of all trips in the data set are undertaken when trains
operate at headways less than 1.67 minutes, and so the plateau in model 1 is most likely
representing the transition from bunched train operations in highly congested conditions at
headways shorter than the published minimum, to regular train operations in line with the
published schedules. Considering this, it is likely that the addition of covariates representing
headway regularity and passenger volumes in models 2, 3, and 5 has addressed potential
confounding with headway, and corrected the plateau observed in model 1.
    For the final model form 5, the explanatory power of the model is relatively low as
                    2
indicated by the Radj.  and Dexplained values of 0.16. Though we have included 10 additional
covariates that are most likely to have a confounding effect on the relationship between access
times and headways, there may be additional missing covariates that explain the additional
variation in waiting and walking times. It should be noted that though the explanatory
power of the model is relatively low, this does not impact the primary motive of the model,
which is to quantify the relationship between access times and headways. As mentioned,
the headway covariate is significant at a level of α = 0.001, furthermore, we have included
10 additional supply/demand covariates that are most likely to have a confounding effect
                                              19
on headway, and we have demonstrated that the relationship between headway and access

    It should be noted that another reason for the relatively low explanatory power may be
a consequence of the Oyster card timestamp precision. The Oyster timestamps are accurate
to one minute only. As information on the magnitude of the inaccuracy for each timestamp
is unknown, the inaccuracy can be assumed to follow a uniform random distribution between
0 and 59 seconds, and this is captured by the random error term of the access time regres-
sion model. Furthermore, any random error in the passenger to train assignment algorithm
would also be captured in the random error term of the model. It is worth highlighting that
potential impacts on the random error term do not impact the magnitude of the param-
eter estimates, and so the relationship between access times and headways would remain
robust. We acknowledge that if there are systematic errors in the timestamp precision and
assignment, this would result in biased estimates.

    Comparing the results of the two sets of models, it can be seen that the effects of the
different time periods present in the environmental models in Figure 7 are not observed in
the base models, and across all models, the maximum values of the environmental models
are greater than the corresponding maximum values of the base models. The results from
the environmental and base models can therefore be considered plausible. The base models
are not influenced by differing levels of congestion across the peak and off-peak time periods
as the functions represent the sole influence of headway on base wait times (please refer to
equation 16). The maximum values of the environmental models are higher during the AM
and PM peak periods compared to the inter-peak period, and the functions cross 0.5 at longer
headways during the peak periods compared to the inter-peak, implying a lower proportion
of non-random arrivals during the peaks. The results for the environmental models therefore
represent the differences in the environmental components of marginal wait times and walk
times, including congestion and individual-specific behaviours, at different times of the day.
    We now turn our attention to the form of the environmental and base models in repre-
senting marginal wait time patterns. As discussed in the literature review, the prevailing
                                                                                  27
consensus is that for high frequency services, passengers arrive randomly with a uniform
random distribution. Under perfectly random conditions, the average passenger wait time
(w) is equivalent to half of the headway between trains, i.e. w = 0.5h = 0.5f −1 , where h
is train headway and f is the frequency of services. Under such conditions the marginal
impact of headway on waiting time should remain 0.5, no matter what the initial frequency
is. As shown in both the environmental and base models, when headways are less than
approximately 2-3 minutes, the impact of a change in headways is much greater than half of
the headway. This outcome could be a consequence of station congestion and the possibility
of denied boarding during these high frequency operating periods.
    In low frequency periods, the literature suggests that passenger wait times are less than
half of the headway between trains i.e. w < 0.5h. In both the environmental and base
models, when headways are longer than approximately 2-3 minutes, the models predict that
the average passenger saves less time than half of the headway. This finding is in line with
the literature for wait times at long headways, albeit at lower values of headway compared
to the conventional rule of thumb of a 10 minute headway transition value. Across all time
periods, passengers arrivals tend to become non-random at a headway between a lower bound
of 1.95 minutes and an upper bound of 2.35 minutes. As a result, 55%-72% of passengers
experience marginal wait times less than half of the headway. For those passengers that
experience marginal wait times less than 0.5, the average marginal wait time lies between
0.30-0.36, or approximately one third of the operating headway.
    Wait times below half of the headway are likely to be caused by the fact that, on average,
passengers do not arrive at the station randomly. A potential explanation for this could be
that when the system is operating at longer headways, the system is also less congested,
and this enables passengers to move more freely through stations. The reduced levels of
congestion may better enable passengers to speed up when they are aware of an approaching
train, and then board the train with minimal wait time at the platform. Awareness may
arise from real-time train arrival information on digital information display boards at the
station prior to entry, from mobile phone applications, and/or from hearing and seeing the
train approach en route to the platform.
    The maximum values of the functions represent the maximum marginal wait time of
passengers, and for all functions, this occurs when trains operate at the shortest headways.
In this region, travel conditions are most likely to be congested with high levels of passen-
ger demand possibly resulting in denied boarding and/or issues with train reliability with
possible train bunching. For the base models, the maximum value across all time periods
is 0.76 while for the environmental models the maximum values across all time periods is
1.17. The interpretation of this estimate is that if the operator manages to reduce the peak
headway by one second, it leads to between 0.76-1.17 times savings in waiting time for the
average passenger.

                                                          32
6. Conclusions
    In this paper, the impact of headways on passenger wait times for urban metro systems is
derived. The analysis is undertaken for single-line trips excluding route choice and transfer
options on three lines of the London Underground metro system. First, passengers are
assigned to trains to enable the total journey times recorded by the AFC data on passenger
trips to be decomposed into components. After assignment, the access time component is
calculated, and a semiparametric regression analysis is then performed with access time set
as the response variable. Headway is specified as an explanatory variable, and additional
explanatory variables are included to capture the effects of headway variance, static station
and network characteristics, passenger demand, and individual passenger characteristics.
    Accounting for potential confounding between the walk and wait time components of
access time, we isolate the unbiased effect of headways on marginal passenger wait times via
an exposure-response model framework. The results show that wait times are more impacted
by changes to service frequency during high frequency operating periods, compared to low
frequency operating periods. The results indicate that passenger arrival patterns transition
from random to non-random as headways increase, and that marginal wait times for non-
random arrivals are equivalent to approximately one third of the headway between trains.
This observed behaviour could be a result of passengers consulting train arrival information
prior to boarding, and if so, this would demonstrate that generalised travel time savings can
be achieved through providing improved passenger information.
    The empirical results are then applied in a numerical exercise of supply optimisation to
show that the assumption behind the headway-wait time relationship has crucial impact on
the socially optimal frequency. The evidence on non-random arrivals implies that the existing
literature might underestimate the optimal peak service frequencies and overestimate those
in low demand periods. Under welfare maximising service provision, the new evidence on
non-random arrivals questions the prevalence of density economies in operating costs with
respect to the final output (i.e. ridership), and thus also the justification for high public
transport subsidies.
    The exposure-response relationship of marginal wait times and headway presented here
can be applied in other contexts in future work. For example, the data could be segmented
at route, station, and line levels or by passenger demographics to obtain wait time patterns
at a more disaggregate level. At a wider spatial scale, the analysis could be undertaken at
a whole-of-network level to enable comparisons of passenger arrival patterns and wait times
across different transit networks.
